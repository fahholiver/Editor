The wind. At the top of the clouds it's blowing at around three hundred kilometres an hour, fast enough to drag you all the way around the planet in four days.

Then come the clouds. They start about seventy kilometres up, and they're not water. They're sulfuric acid. Twenty-five kilometres of it, straight down.

Fall through them and the air gets heavy. Venus's sky is almost all carbon dioxide, and there's so much of it that at the ground, it presses on you ninety-two times harder than Earth's air does. Submarines feel that nearly a kilometre under the ocean.

And the heat. The surface is about four hundred and sixty-five degrees Celsius. Day or night, it barely changes. That's hotter than Mercury, which sits much closer to the Sun. Hot enough to melt lead.

We know this because we sent machines. In 1982, the Soviet lander Venera 13 touched down here. It was built to survive thirty-two minutes. It held on for a hundred and twenty-seven, long enough to send back colour photos of the ground, before Venus cooked it.

And if you somehow stood here, things would feel wrong. Venus takes longer to spin once than to go all the way around the Sun. And it spins backwards, so the Sun rises in the west.

The ground might not even be still. In 2023, scientists going back through old radar images found a volcanic vent that changed shape in just eight months. Venus may still be erupting.

Here's the strange part, though. Go back up to about fifty kilometres, and the air pressure drops to roughly what you're feeling right now. It's one of the most Earth-like places anywhere in the solar system. NASA has even studied airships that could carry people there, floating above the acid.
