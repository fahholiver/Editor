# Orbit script formula

These are patterns from a 7.5M-view deep-sea explainer, adapted for space. Reuse the techniques; never reuse anyone's wording.
Read this before writing any script.

## Length
- Channel videos: 8–11 minutes (about 1,100–1,550 words). Videos over 8 minutes can carry mid-roll ads, and the format holds attention for that long.
- Shorts or demos: 2–3 minutes, using the same beats but fewer of them.

## Timing map (from the reference video: 10:00, about 1,550 words, about 155 words a minute)
| Time | Beat |
|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | Cold open: the rule, then a sensation breaks it, then the reveal. Plant the mystery. |
| 0:30–1:05 | Correct the misconception, three superlatives, the promise |
| 1:05–1:45 | Size and look, each number with a comparison |
| 1:45–3:00 | What it does to you, part 1 (question → fact → proof → joke) |
| 3:00–4:00 | How it works, then **pay off the planted mystery at about 3:50** |
| 4:00–5:00 | The most unsettling part, recent research (2024), **"picture it again" at the midpoint** |
| 5:00–6:05 | True story with dates (two cases) |
| 6:05–7:10 | The survival game: four options knocked down, then the verdict |
| 7:10–8:50 | The reversal: it's not what you thought, the real danger, a strange fact |
| 8:50–9:25 | Why it matters (the hidden good it does) |
| 9:25–10:00 | Closing callback to the rule, outro question |

Rhythm: a new section about every 60 seconds, each opened by a question; a joke every 45–60 seconds; the hook's payoff about 35–40 % of the way in.

## The beats, in order

**1. Cold open: the rule, then the break (0:00–0:30).**
Start with "Picture this." Put the viewer somewhere specific with a comfortable belief ("You've done your homework. Venus is just clouds. Clouds can't hurt you. That's the rule."). Then break the rule with a *sensation* before an explanation ("Then the suit starts to tick. Not a sound, a feeling, like fingernails on the visor."). End on a one-line reveal that makes it personal ("That's the acid. And it's already eating your suit.").
- **Plant a mystery sensation in the hook, and pay it off later** ("Remember that ticking from the start? That was…"). This one callback keeps people watching to find out what it was.

**2. Correct the misconception (0:30–1:00).**
"When most people think of the deadliest planet, they think of ___." Admit what's true about that ("And yes…"), then put the subject "in a different category" with two or three superlatives: hottest planet, heaviest air, a probe that lasted two hours.

**3. The promise.**
Say the premise out loud: "So let's look at what would actually kill you first." The viewer now knows exactly what they're waiting for.

**4. Every number gets a comparison you can feel.**
Never leave a number alone. Compare it to an everyday object ("about the weight of six elephants", "hot enough to melt a frying pan's worth of lead"), or to the viewer's own body ("you'd struggle to hold your breath for a minute in a swimming pool").

**5. Open every section with the question the viewer is already asking.**
"Which raises the obvious question: how do you get down there?" "So how did anyone ever measure this?" "But has anything ever actually survived it?" The question is the transition. Avoid "next" and "another".

**6. A light joke after each scary fact, about every 45–60 seconds.**
One dry line, not a bit. Good jokes put the danger into everyday life ("Going lower isn't escaping. It's walking into the oven." "That wasn't a malfunction, it was a complaint."). Humour gives the viewer a breather so the next fact hits harder.

**7. Put the viewer back in the scene about halfway through.**
"So picture it again. You're hanging in the clouds, and…" Restate their situation with everything they've learned since.

**8. One true story with a date.**
A real mission, probe, astronaut or historic event, told as a short story: what they expected, what happened, how it ended (Venera 13 in 1982, Apollo 13, Mars Climate Orbiter, the Challenger seals). Dates and names build trust.

**9. Recent research.**
Add one finding from the last 1–3 years, with the year and who found it ("In 2023, scientists going back through old radar images…"). It makes the video feel current, not like a textbook.

**10. Be honest about uncertainty.**
Say it when an idea is unproven ("That's never been confirmed. But the fact scientists had to seriously ask says a lot."). Use "by one widely cited estimate" for contested numbers. This builds trust and costs no drama.

**11. The survival game (near the end).**
"If you were really there, what could you actually do?" Give three or four options, and knock each one down with a fact and a joke: "You could fly higher… You could dig in… You could wait for night… You could call for help…" End on the verdict: "So the honest answer is: nothing."

**12. The reversal.**
"It's easy to see ___ as a monster. But here's the surprising part." Flip the danger: the dangerous place is also useful, beautiful, or almost like home. For example, the most Earth-like air in the solar system floats above Venus's acid, or the planet that kills you also explains Earth's climate.

**13. A closing callback to the rule.**
Repeat the opening belief, admit it's mostly true, then finish with the place still being out there, indifferent: "It just has better things to do than you."

**14. Outro (one or two lines).**
Ask a specific question ("Which planet should we survive next? I read every comment."). On a channel with a library, add one line pointing to another video.

## Writing for the edit
- **Every joke names its gag.** In your planning notes (not the narration), write the visual for each joke, for example: "receipt: VENUS DINER, SPACESUIT ×1 … TOTAL: ALL OF IT". If you can't picture a gag, rewrite the joke.
- Plan where YOU appears in every section (astronaut, stick figure, x-ray).
- Plan the last shot as a mirror of the first.
- Mark uncertain claims so the edit can label them.

## Line-level rules
- Second person throughout ("you"), present tense in scenes.
- Short spoken sentences. Fragments are fine ("Not see it. Feel it.").
- Write numbers the way they're said ("four hundred and sixty-five degrees").
- No em dashes, no "not just X but Y", no "in conclusion", no filler.
- Run the script through the humanizer before showing it to the user.

|---|---|
| Rule then break | astronaut + `you` arrow, then a `flash` and a red `caption` for the sensation |
| Superlatives | `pill` stack, last one `tone: red` |
| Comparison | `lineup`, `fit`, `depth`, or a `big` number with `sub` |
| Question transitions | `chapter` card, or a yellow `big` question |

| True story | `tag` with name and year, `timer`, `probe`/`rocket`, `evidence` image |
| Survival game | one `pill` per option, each then hit with a red `stamp` ("NOPE", "WORSE", "MELTED") |
| Reversal | a crossfade to a calm, brighter palette, and a green `stamp` |
