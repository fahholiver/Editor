You will never see a square window on a passenger jet. And the reason cost lives.

1952. Britain launches the de Havilland Comet, the world's first jet airliner. Fast. High. The future.

In January 1954, a Comet breaks apart over the sea near Elba, Italy. Three months later, another falls near Naples.

So investigators sink a whole Comet in a giant water tank, and pressurize it again and again. Thousands of fake flights.

Then it cracks. Right at the sharp corner of a window cutout.

Here's why. Every flight, the cabin puffs up like a balloon, then shrinks when you land. That stress flows through the metal skin. Curves spread it out. Sharp corners pile it into one tiny spot. Flight after flight, the metal gets tired. Engineers call it metal fatigue.

So now, every window is rounded.

Next time you fly, look at your window. That curve isn't style. It's a lesson.
